Many parents living in Canada, France or elsewhere abroad share the same experience: Arabic is sometimes spoken at home, but the children answer in English or French. They understand a little, read with difficulty and do not write. Passing on the Arabic language is entirely possible, provided you approach it with method and consistency. Here is our guide.

Why teach your child Arabic?

  • Family ties: talking with grandparents and understanding cousins during holidays.
  • Access to the Quran: reading and understanding the text in its original language.
  • An asset for the future: Arabic is spoken by hundreds of millions of people and is valued in many careers.
  • A more agile mind: bilingual children often develop great intellectual flexibility.

When to start?

The earlier the better, but it is never too late. Before age 6, children absorb the language orally through songs, stories and simple conversations. Around 6–7, when they learn to read at school, they can also tackle the Arabic alphabet. Teenagers often progress very quickly because they are more autonomous.

Consistency beats intensity: two 30-minute sessions a week for a year are worth more than a one-week intensive course.

Where to begin: reading, writing or speaking?

  1. Speaking first: everyday words, greetings, useful phrases at home.
  2. The alphabet and reading: letters, their shapes by position, short vowels.
  3. Writing: copying, then writing words and short sentences.
  4. Comprehension: short texts, illustrated stories, dialogues.

Modern Standard Arabic (fusha) is the language of writing, books and the Quran, while dialects are used for family conversation. The two complement each other.

Organising the week in Canada

School timetables leave little room, so short, fixed slots work best. In Quebec and Ontario, Saturday and Sunday mornings match the afternoon in Tunisia, which makes live lessons with an Arabic-speaking teacher easy without staying up late. Ten minutes of reading in the evening between lessons makes all the difference.

Keeping motivation over time

  • Link the language to enjoyable moments: cartoons, songs, games.
  • Create rituals: a bedtime story in Arabic, a few new words at dinner.
  • Celebrate progress: the first letter written, the first text read alone.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take for a child to read Arabic?

With two sessions a week, most children decode simple words within a few months and read short texts in about a year.

Do we need to speak Arabic at home?

It is not essential, but a few minutes of daily practice clearly speed up progress.

My child mixes Arabic and English. Is that a problem?

No, it is a normal stage of bilingualism that fades naturally as both languages strengthen.
